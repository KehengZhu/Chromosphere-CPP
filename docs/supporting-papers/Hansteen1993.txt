A NEW INTERPRETATION OF THE REDSHIFT OBSERVED IN OPTICALLY THIN
TRANSITION REGION LINES

Viggo H. Hansteen¹
Institute of Theoretical Astrophysics, University of Oslo, P.O. Box 1029, Blindern N-0315, Oslo, Norway
Received 1992 April 9; accepted 1992 July 17

ABSTRACT

We propose that the pervasive redshift observed in transition region spectral lines is caused by downward
propagating acoustic waves. The waves are assumed to be generated in the corona as a result of nanoflares or
some other form of episodic heating. The dynamic response of a coronal loop to energy released as heat near
the loop apex is studied by solving the hydrodynamic equations numerically, consistently including the effects
of nonequilibrium ionization of the radiative losses and on the internal energy. We find that the radiative loss
curve may change by a factor of 2 during the loop evolution as a result of flows and waves. Spectral line
intensities and line profiles are computed in accordance with the numerical model for the resonance lines of
C IV, O IV, O VI, and Ne VIII. The three lower temperature lines display an average redshift on the order of I
km s⁻¹ while the high-temperature line (Ne VIII) displays little average shift. The largest line shifts occur as
the loop undergoes transient heating. A simple analytical analysis is performed to isolate the physical effects
relevant to the line formation process. We find that the amplitude of the line shift depends on the character-
istic time scale for ionization of the radiating ion as well as the global loop parameters; the time scales for
loop cooling, the maximum temperature, and the base pressure. The importance of nonequilibrium ionization
in the line-forming process will also have impact on the interpretation of the emission measure derived from
ionization data.

Subject headings: line: formation — radiative transfer — Sun: transition region

1. INTRODUCTION

Observations of spectral lines formed in the solar transition
region have provided clues to discriminating between
mechanisms responsible for locating the solar corona. There are
several advantages in using observations of spectral lines
formed in this region. Transition region resonance lines are in
the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) where data with high spatial
resolution of 1″ (700 km at the Sun), spectral resolution of 0.05
Å and temporal resolution 5 s are available from the High-
Resolution Telescope and Spectrograph (Bartoe & Brueckner
1975). As the lines are optically thin, we may force the solving
the radiative transport equation in mapping the observed line
characteristics to plasma parameters. The transition region has
shorter cooling times than the corona and is thus able to
rapidly respond to changes in the energy balance. The major
difficulty in understanding the observations is the compli-
cated geometry and time-variability of the transition region
such that a statistical approach to observation analysis must
often be followed. The transport of energy into the corona will
affect the transition region both as the energy passes upward
from the convection zone and as "overflow" energy is trans-
ported downward. In this paper we will concentrate on the
observational aspects of the transport of energy by downward
propagating acoustic waves.

There are two basic coronal heating theories currently in
vogue, namely, resonant absorption of magnetohydrodynamic
waves and nanoflare heating (see e.g., Ulinschenkski, Priest, &
Rosner 1991). The studies of resonant absorption have focused
on Alfvén waves generated in deeper layers that become
trapped in resonant cavities in the corona. The observational
signature of this process should (1) be the signature of stand-

ing or propagating Alfvén waves, with periods in the range
5–300 s in order to fit into coronal loops, (2) the signature of
any downward propagating signal from the dissipation
process itself. We expect that the former signature will domi-
nate the line profile in the transition region unless the dissi-
pation mechanism has a time dependence resembling
nanofares as described below.

Nanoflare heating of the corona depends on the low-
frequency random motions of the photospheric granulation.
The motions of the footpoints of coronal tubes by the
photospheric plasma continually deforms the coronal mag-
netic field. This continual deformation introduces a large
number of tangential discontinuities which, upon reaching
some critical strength, dissipate through rapid reconnection
(Parker 1988). Coronal heating is achieved by the sum of the
heat deposited by all these small reconnection events. Each
event has a duration on the order of a few seconds and a spatial
scale below present observational limits. Parker (1991) esti-
mates the energy of one nanoflare to be ~10²³–10²⁴ ergs. Re-
leasing a large amount of energy in a small volume and in the
course of a few seconds will evict a material magnetohydro-
dynamic (MHD) wave modes in the low β plasma. The
signature of the nanoflare heating mechanism in transition
region lines will consist of the transition region response to the
downward propagating waves. The signature will be modu-
lated by the global heating and cooling of the loop as the loop
responds to the heat injected by the nanofares.

The solar chromosphere-corona transition region is highly
structured, both spatially and temporally. Intensity observa-
tions by Dere, Bartoe, & Brueckner (1984), Porter et al. (1987),
and Habbal (1990) indicate that the dominant portion of the
transition region radiation arises in regions of variable emis-
sion. There are, however, two characteristics of the observa-
tions that seem to be invariant from region to region and from

net redshift of spectral lines. It should be mentioned that the
redshift decreases toward the limb (Dere et al. 1984; Doschek
et al. 1976), which indicates that the motions are primarily vertical. The invariant shape of the

several other stars (e.g., Jordan & Linsky 1987; Ayres, Jensen,

Analysis of spectral line profiles shows that transition region
lines display a net redshift (e.g. Doschek, Feldman, & Bohlin
1976; Dere et al. 1984; Athay & Dere 1989; Hassler, Rottman,
& Orrall 1991). The observed mean redshift is typically 5 km
s⁻¹ for C IV z1548 formed at 10⁴ K but varies considerably.
The amplitude of the shift is largest for lines formed at lower
temperatures and chromospheric lines display little net shift
(see Samain 1991). There are few observations of higher tem-
perature lines but there is evidence that the net redshift is
reduced or disappears in the upper transition region. Doschek
et al. (1976) report no substantial downflow for O V z1218
formed at 2.5 × 10⁵ K. Since the wavelength determination of
this oxygen line may be influenced by Ly z(2116), it is perhaps
more significant that Hassler et al. (1991) report a downflow of
0 ± 4 km s⁻¹ for the Ne VIII 2770 line formed at 8 × 10⁵ K.
The center-to-limb variation of the redshifts seem to indicate
that the motions are primarily vertical, but a horizontal flow
component is also present. There is no clear correlation
between the intensity of the line and the line shift, but there is
correlation between intensity and line shift and between line width
and line shift. At line shift correlations (McWhirter et al. 1994).

The existence of the solar wind makes it evident that there is
no net flow of mass from the chromosphere and explanations
for the net redshifts have invoked "collection" effects in the
radiative or thermal properties of gas flowing up. Perennian &
Kopp (1977) and later Athay & Dere (1982) hypothesized that the redshifts were
caused by the return of spectacular material. Alternatively, the
observations signatures were interpreted as upflow due from
through coronal loops (e.g., Boris & Mariska 1982). McCly-
mont & Craig (1986, 1987) and Mariska (1988) showed that
asymmetric heating of coronal loops may drive the flow. Both
ideas seem conflicting; however, the observations reported by
Athay & Dere (1989) seem inconsistent with a steady flow of
material returning to the chromosphere.

In this paper we propose that the net redshifts may be
explained as a consequence of nanoflare coronal heating.
Several of the wave modes generated by a nanoflare near the
apex of a coronal loop will propagate along the loop through
the transition region. Alfvén waves are noncompressive, and it is thus difficult to separate upward propagating
from downward propagating Alfvén waves. This is particularly
true if we have observations only in the transition region where
do we expect, however, that the presence of Alfvén waves will
broaden the transition region spectral lines (see Hansteen &
Malty 1990). On the other hand, propagating compressive
waves will cause spectral line asymmetries and line shifts in the
"direction" of wave propagation (Erickson & Maltby 1967;
McWhirter & Wilson 1974; Brecht, McWhirter, & Winters
1975; Goutthebroz 1989). Nanoflare-generated waves are of
high amplitude and may evolve into shock waves. Shocks lead
to changes in line emission strength (e.g., Elzner 1975; Poletto
et al. 1974; Flower & Pineau des Forêts 1983) and also alter the line
profile (Hansteen 1991). Thus we should expect a net redshift
resulting from compressive waves originating in the corona
and propagating down through the transition region. The
focus of this paper will therefore be on the effects of the com-

pressive waves that result from the episodic release of energy
near the apex of coronal loops. For reasons of computational
expediency we consider a one-dimensional loop with strong
magnetic field and restrict our attention to the slow-mode
waves propagating along the magnetic field in this low β
plasma. Other wave modes may be important in a further
development of this idea.

2. LOOP MODEL

2.1. Equations and Assumptions

The loop into which the nanoflares are introduced consists
of a one-dimensional semicircular geometry of length l and
constant cross section containing a plasma of hydrogen,
helium, carbon, oxygen, and neon (all photospheric)
abundances (Grevesse 1984). We solve the equations of mass,
momentum, and energy conservation to obtain the run of
density (ρ), velocity (u), temperature (T), and population den-
sities (n_i) of elements i in ionization states j along the loop as a
function of time. The variable s is the length along the loop
while t is the time.

We make the assumption that all species have the same flow
speed and the same temperature. In a collisionless plasma
acoustic waves are heavily Landau damped; however, at the
densities (ρ > 10⁻⁹ g cm⁻³) and temperatures (T > 2.5 × 10⁶ K)
we find the ion-ion collision frequency ν_i sufficiently larger
than the wave frequency to ensure a collisionally dominated
plasma. This also applies to the electron-ion collision fre-
quency ν_e; we may safely assume that ion and electron tem-
peratures are equal on the time scales of interest in this study. A
magnetic field of large enough to ensure a low β plasma in
the low-density transition region and corona is assumed, in
this case, acoustic waves propagate along the magnetic field
and a one-dimensional approach is justified, since we are
ignoring the Alfvén and fast mode waves.

In the observer's frame the ionization rate equation for
element i and ionization state j, is written

∂n_{ij}/∂t + ∂/∂z(n_{ij}u) = n_i[n_j - C_{j-1} - n_j(C_j + z_j) + n_{j+1} z_{j+1}]. (1)

The ionization and recombination rates are represented,
respectively, by C_j and z_j. It is assumed that local processes
dominate the ionization rate; the rates are mainly functions of
temperature except the electronic recombination rates which
have a slight dependence on electron density, n_e. Photoioniza-
tions are ignored, although they presumably play an important
role in the ionization state of hydrogen below T ≈ 2 × 10⁴ K
and of helium below T ≈ 8 × 10⁴ K.

The spontaneous and dielectronic recombination rates are
calculated according to Mazzotta et al. (1973, 1976), the
collisional excitation ionization rate is obtained from Bell et al. (1982), and the collisional excitation
rates are taken from the compendium of Mertz et al. (1980).
The equation ensuring the conservation of mass is written

∂ρ/∂t + ∂/∂z(ρu) = 0. (2)

The momentum equation is written

ρ(∂u/∂t + u∂u/∂z) = -∂/∂z(p + Q) - ρg_z, (3)

where p = nkT_e is the gas pressure, and g_z the absolute value of
the component of gravity parallel with the magnetic field (z).
We have also allowed for an "artificial viscosity" term, ∂Q/∂z,
which is discussed in the Appendix. The energy equation is
written

ρ(∂/∂t(ρe) + ∂/∂z(ρue)) + (p + Q)∂u/∂z = -∂F_c/∂z + S - L_r. (4)

The denotes F_c, and the divergence of the conductive
heat flux, the radiative loss per unit volume and time L_r, and the heating rate S. The
internal energy e is calculated as the sum of the thermal and
internal energy including only ionization states, the contribu-
tion from the radiation energy is negligible.

We consider the radiative losses from the transition region
to be pushed upward and downward. This should be a reasonable assumption
with the exception of the radiation loss from neutral hydrogen
in the Lyman α. In this paper heat is added to the footpoints
of the loop where hydrogen becomes neutral to balance the Lyman
radiative losses and maintain a reasonable chromospheric tem-
perature. Kuin & Poland (1991) have presented an alternate
approach to treating the Lyman radiative losses. We have not
used their method, as it does not allow for a consistent treatment of the radiation
and time-dependent ionization state of hydrogen, the impor-
tance of which has been pointed out by Carlsson & Stein
(1993).

Optically thin radiative losses are computed consistently
with the hydrodynamic equations by summing the individual
loss rates of the various processes. In the transition region the
most important processes are the resonance line radiation of
ionized metals, radiative recombinations, and at higher tem-
perature (T > 10⁶ K) electron-ion free-free radiation. We
parameterize the radiative loss curve as

L_r = n_e ∑ n_i ν_i C_u + L_rsx + L_rff, (5)

where the sum is over all elements and ionization stages, with
population densities n_i, transition energies h_ν, and excitation
rates n_i, C_u. The quantities L_rsx and L_rff represent the losses
from recombination and bremsstrahlung. In the present paper
the resonance line losses from hydrogen, helium, carbon,
oxygen, and neon along with loss from electron-ion
trating are included. This gives a fairly good representation of
the radiative loss curve for the temperature range 2 × 10⁴
K < T < 3 × 10⁶ K. The omission of iron from the radiative
losses leads to an underestimate of the losses at temperatures
above 3 × 10⁶ K (Landoni & Mongonrot-Fossi 1990). This
will in turn lead to a greater conductive flux and therefore a
larger temperature gradient in the transition region where
would be the case if iron included in the radiative losses. We
find that the computed radiative loss curve Φ(T) = L_r/n_e/ρ,
varies by more than a factor of 2 in the transition region during
the time evolution of the model calculation as a result of non-
equilibrium ionization as described in § 3. Figure 1 shows this
variation, where the maximum and minimum loss curves Φ(T)
are plotted. This variation is much larger than the 10% gain in
accuracy achieved by including ions such as silicon and nitro-
gen in equilibrium.

The conductive flux F_c is assumed to run parallel with the
magnetic field and is computed in this paper according to
Spitzer (1962),

F_c = -κ_0 T^{5/2} dT/dz, (6)

where κ_0 = 1.1 × 10^{-6} ergs cm^{-1} s^{-1} K^{-7/2}. This should be
an adequate approximation for the coronal and upper tran-
sition region part of the loop, but may have to be revised for
the lower transition region (e.g., Fontella, Avrett, & Loeser
1990) at if the temperature gradient becomes extremely large
(e.g., Shoub 1982, 1983).

It should be pointed out that the energy balance is coupled
to the ionization state of the plasma both through the calcu-
lation of the internal energy and through the radiative losses.
For several of the relevant ions the characteristic times of col-
lisional ionization and recombination are long compared to
the dynamic time scales of the plasma, and we cannot expect
the plasma to be in the ionization equilibrium.

2.2. Initial Loop

The initial loop was set up with footpoint pressure of p_E =
0.7 dyn cm⁻² and a temperature of T = 1.5 × 10⁴ K. Since
photoionizations are ignored hydrogen is some 65% neutral at
this temperature, giving a footpoint electron density of n_e =
8 × 10⁹ cm⁻³. In the "chromospheric" region stretching up
for s = 1000 km, heating and radiation are balanced. This is
achieved by adding a heating term S = 3 × 10^{-3} n_μ to the
energy equation; since this scales approximately as the radi-
ative losses (mainly from Lyman) the chromospheric temperature
is constant until the transition region temperature rise is
encountered. Figure 2 shows the model loop at the onset of the
calculation, where the temperature, electron density, and gas
pressure are plotted in the left panel, while the velocity is
plotted in the right panel. All these variables are plotted as a
function of distance z along the loop, which is given a length of
l = 20 Mm.

a maximum temperature of T ≈ 3 × 10⁵. The transition region
has a temperature scale height H_s ~ 180 km as determined by
the divergence of the thermal conduction and the radiative loss
terms which dominate the energy equation in this temperature
range. The heating function used to heat the chromosphere
plays a small role in the transition region and is unimportant

We reconsider the emission of optically thin resonance lines
from multiple ionized metals formed in the transition region in
the presence of atmospheric perturbations. To a good approx-
imation (e.g., Jordan & Wilson 1971) the statistical equilibrium
between the upper (u) and lower (l) level of a transition region
resonance line is determined locally by the balance between
collisional excitation and spontaneous de-excitation

n_l n_e C_u = n_u A_u1. (7)

Here n_l and n_u are the lower level and upper level number
densities, n_e is the electron density, C_u is the
electron impact collision rate, and A_u1 the Einstein probability
for spontaneous de-excitation. Since we are considering reso-
nance lines and n_e C_u ≪ A_u1, we may identify the population of
the lower level with the total population of the radiating ion,
i.e., n_l ≈ n_ij.

The emergent intensity from the transition region and
corona, for an optically thin transition of energy hν_ul and an
emission profile equal to the absorption profile φ_ν may be
written

I_ν = (hν_ul)/(4π) ∫₀^l φ_ν n_u n_j C_u ds. (8)

where we have employed the assumptions above, and we
assume an area fill factor f_a = 1. The integration is carried
out along the line of sight, here represented by path length

The absorption (and emission) profile is in the case of
thermal Doppler broadening given by

φ_ν(z, n) = (1)/(π^{1/2} Δν_D) exp[-(Δν - ν_u cos θ/c)²/(Δν_D)²], (9)

where Δν = ν - ν_ul is the distance from the rest frequency, and
θ = φ - φ_E is the angle, and φ_E is the angle along the line of
sight. All variation of the absorption profile is along the length
variable z. The thermal broadening corresponds to a width

Δν_D = (ν_ul)/(c) √(2kT)/(m_i), (10)

where m_i is the mass of the radiating ion.

With a Maxwellian electron distribution function the collision
rate is a function of the temperature alone

C_u(T) = C_u^0 (1)/(η_l) T^{1/2} exp(-h_ul/(kT))Γ(T), (11)

where Γ, the Maxwellian-averaged collision strength (Seaton
1962), is a slowly varying function of the temperature. η_l is the
ionization potential of the lower level, e.g., ≈ 863.3 × 10^{-12} erg K^{-1}.

In order to isolate the physical effect of the compressive
wave on the intensity and line profile, the population of the
radiating ion is rewritten as

n_ij = x_ij (n_t)/(n_H) n_H, (12)

where n_i is the total density of the atom to which the radiating
ion belongs, n_H is the total hydrogen density, and n_H is the
total hydrogen density, and x_ij = n_ij/n_i is the ionic concentra-
tion. The region of line formation is the region along the line of
sight where the function

ψ(T) = x_ij exp(-h_ul/(kT))T_s^{1/2} (13)

is significantly different from zero. The function ψ(T) generally
has a sharp maximum near the temperature of maximum
concentration, and the extent of the region of line formation
is thus a good indicator measure of the temperature gradient.
This has been used in emission measure analysis of transition
region lines (e.g., Petsuch 1964; Jordan & Wilson 1971). Inte-
grating over line frequency and using the expressions given
above the integrated line intensity is seen to vary as

I ∝ n_e n_ij x_ij C_u Δz, (14)

where the variables are evaluated at some appropriate place in
the line-forming region.

The response of the optically thin transition region line
profile and intensity depends on how the densities described
above correlate in the presence of a compressive perturbation.
The basic effects that outlined by considering a sinusoidal
progressive sound wave with frequency ω propagating along
the loop in the s-direction. The particle velocity v_x varies in
phase with the pressure perturbation Δp and the density per-
turbation Δρ

u_z/c_s = (Δp)/(ρ) (Δρ)/(ρ) sin[ω(t - z/c_s)], (15)

where Δp_ic is the amplitude of the pressure perturbation and
the sound speed c_s is given by c_s = √(γp/ρ), where γ is the usual
ratio of the specific heats (c_p/c_v). The phase relation between
the density and velocity perturbation will cause the inten-
sity to vary with the loss-of-sight velocity, leading to a net shift
of the spectral line if we integrate over a wave period Δz'=
2π/ω. Thus, in the absence of other effects we expect redshifted
transition region lines if pressure perturbations originate in the
corona and propagate down the chromosphere. On the other
hand, blueshifted spectral lines are expected should remnants of
the acoustic waves which presumably heat the lower chromo-
sphere propagate through the transition region and dominate
other pressure perturbations.

The above discussion was made without reference to depar-
tures from ionization equilibrium and variations in the tem-
perature, which will effect both the intensity integrand in
equation (8) and the extent of the region of line formation. The
effect of nonequilibrium ionization will cause the response of
the spectral line to an acoustic perturbation to be not only a
function of the density and temperature but also of the velocity
and of the history of the plasma. In addition, the waves we are
considering are propagating in a time-varying atmosphere as
the loop heats and cools. The consequences of these nonlinear
effects are taken into account in the numerical calculations.

4. RESULTS

The nanoflare model requires that tangential discontinuities
introduced by the random motions of the loop footpoints
explosively dissipate, releasing 10²³–10²⁴ ergs within a few
seconds in a small region. The physics of time-dependent
reconnection is a difficult subject, but perturbations introduced
by this dissipation are expected to propagate away from the
reconnection region with velocities on the order of the Alfvén
speed v_A as well as the sound speed c_s. As noted in § 2.1
Alfvén fast mode waves are ignored. It is imagined that the energy
release, momentum will be injected directly to the
plasma in the reconnection event. Since we are interested in
effects occurring at large distances from the nanofares we
assume that the momentum released is thermalized before
reaching scales comparable to that of the numerical grid.

The nanofares are modeled here by introducing energy in a
randomly located computational cell confined to the upper
third of the loop, centered on the loop apex. The amount of
energy in a single nanoflare is computed from a truncated
Gaussian distribution around a chosen average energy, each
energy released using exponential decay within a few seconds
from zero to this value. The interval between nanofares is
Poisson-random around a given mean interval between explo-
sions.

Let us now consider the response of transition region spec-
tral lines to the nanofares released in the upper (coronal) por-
tions of the loop. In the model described below the atmosphere
is cycled through a heating phase lasting 120 s where nano-
flares are set off in succession, followed by a cooling phase of
250 s where no nanofares are set off. The individual flare has
an average energy release of 10²⁴ ergs ~ 10⁻⁴ ergs cm⁻³ a,
where a is the loop cross section. Since we are considering a
constant cross section loop we will for simplicity set a = 1 cm²
in the following. The nanoflare energy is inserted into the loop
as heat. We assume that the heat is distributed in one computa-
tional cell of length Δs_n = l_p ≈ 10² km. Each nanoflare is
modeled as having a duration s_n = 5 s, while the average time
between flares is set to 30 s during the heating phase. The

5. DISCUSSION

As nanoflares are inserted into the loop acoustic waves are generated and propagate along the magnetic field. On longer time scales the energy inserted is spread throughout the loop by thermal conduction. Conversely, in a period of no nanoflares the loop cools through thermal conduction and radiative losses. These effects will have an impact on the formation of transition region spectral lines. In this section an attempt is made to isolate the physical processes relevant to line formation through simple analytical arguments.

5.1. Nanoflare-generated Waves

In our calculation a pattern of energy E_p ~ ρ_0 dv_0 a, where a is the loop cross section, generates a thermal wave and an acoustic wave. The thermal wave which initially propagates faster than the acoustic wave spreads the nanoflare energy over a large coronal region. Subsequently as the speed of the thermal wave falls below the speed of sound, the nanoflare energy is transported as an acoustic pulse in both directions away from the site of the nanoflare explosion.

We may estimate the characteristics of the early phases of the thermal wave by assuming that the plasma motion and the density perturbations are unimportant in an energy budget dominated by the conductive flux. As long as thermal conduction dominates the energy balance the energy released in the

nanoflare is conserved within the wavefront defined by the thermal wave. The thermal wave propagates from the position of the nanoflare energy release, z_n, and the temperature behind the nanoflare at z = z_n - s. The conductive flux Q is written in order to conserve energy:

Q = e_n/ρc_v = ∫ T dz ≈ T z_f .                    (18)

The energy equation for the early phase of the thermal wave is set up by Stein & Leibovich & Rautzer (1967) as

∂T/∂t = ∂/∂z(κ_0 T^{5/2} ∂T/∂z)                    (19)

by assuming plasma motion and density perturbations are negligible. Replacing ∂T/∂z by 1/z_f and ∂T/∂t by 1/t_f allows for an estimate of the velocity of the thermal wave front

dz_f/dt ≈ z_f/t ≈ κ_0/ρc_v Q^{α} 1/z_f^2

≈ (κ_0/(ρc_v)^{1/2}) a_n^{1/2} 1/z_f^2 .                    (20)

The acoustic pulses generated by the nanoflares are of fairly high amplitude, with energy on the order P ~ 10 s and wavelengths around λ_c ≈ 10^3 km. These acoustic pulses propagate at a speed on the order of the speed of sound c_s ≈ (γ T/μ)^{1/2} in the heated medium. Initially, the thermal wave precedes the acoustic wave, but it is quickly overtaken. With a nanoflare energy of 10^{-2} - 10^{-5} ergs s^-1 the thermal wave is overtaken after τ_0 ≈ 1 s which corresponds to a front position of z_0 ≈ 100 km. Thus the thermal wave sets the wavelength and period of the following acoustic pulse, erasing information on the spatial and temporal properties of the nanoflare as long as the energy release occurs in a region smaller than 1 and is shorter duration than τ_0. The acoustic pulse is itself a source of an additional thermal wave as it compresses and heats the coronal plasma. Since the thermal conductivity coefficient is nonlinear, the superposition principle does not hold and it becomes impossible to separate the thermal and acoustic waves. After a delay of the order of the thermal wave crossing time the transition region is struck by an acoustic shock pulse followed by increased thermal conduction as the flare energy spreads throughout the corona.

The waves propagate towards the transition region where the rapidly decreasing sound speed causes partial reflection of the wave and displacement of the transition region. In the model presented here we have found that typically 70% of the wave energy is transmitted while the rest is reflected back into the corona. By considering the amount of momentum deposited in the chromosphere by a pulse, we obtain a transition region displacement of some hundreds of kilometers during a pulse period P_a = 10 s. The density and temperature perturbations associated with the pulse passing through the accelerated transition region causes spikes in the contribution function; see Figure 4(b) and Figure 5. These spikes show up in the continuum contribution function for the C IV λ1548 line in Figure 4 and the O IV λ789 line in

Figure 5.

5.2. Loop Heating and Cooling

The combined acoustic/thermal wave heats the corona and leads to an increase in the coronal pressure. The transition region does not rebound to its former position after the wave has passed. The net result of several nanoflares is to drive the transition region into the chromosphere thereby increasing the loop length and the transition region density. At the same time, the temperature gradient in the transition region must increase in order to support the increased thermal conduction from the corona. Let us now consider the low-frequency response of the loop and the line-forming region in the transition region as the loop is heated and cools.

We consider the energy and force balance at a specified temperature in the transition region T_r. The radiative loss Q_r varies by approximately a factor of 2.5 of a result of nonequilibrium ionization described below. The divergence of the conductive flux is roughly given as a function of the loop length l and the maximal coronal temperature T_c, by T_c^{7/2}/l. In the upper transition region, the energy balance is dominated by the radiative losses as well as the decrease in thermal conduction flux. The relative size of these terms will determine whether the transition region is being heated or cooled. If the divergence of the thermal conduction flux is larger than the radiative losses at a given temperature material is heated and flows up through the transition region temperature gradient. Conversely, an excess of radiative losses at a given temperature will cause material to cool and flow down.

The electron density n_e,tr in the transition region will, in tending toward force equilibrium, scale with the coronal pressure; n_e,tr ∝ (p_r/T_tr). Likewise, changes in the loop length l will scale with the coronal pressure with Δp_c ≈ (dp/dz)Δl, where (dp/dz) is the chromospheric pressure gradient. The loop length scales on the order of the chromospheric scale height which is small compared to the loop length. In the context of determining the average divergence of thermal conductive flux at a given coronal pressure, we may consider the loop length to be constant. Thus, assuming that the coronal pressure roughly scales with the coronal temperature T_c, we see that for a loop of given length l and base pressure the mass flow is determined by the evolution of the coronal temperature (pressure).

The relevant time scale for the low-frequency transition region motion and flows given by the characteristic time scale of a coronal loop cooling through thermal conduction

t_cool ∝ κ_0 T_c^{7/2}/l^2 ,                    (21)

or by the time scale of a loop being heated. The latter is determined by the temporal distribution of "spicules" of nanoflares and the time scale of spreading the inserted heat throughout the loop, which is on the order of the sound crossing time. The flows resulting from the heating and cooling cycles of the loop are of fairly small magnitude (u ~ 1 km s^-1 at T ≈ 10^5 K) compared to the impulsive velocities generated by the nanoflares. However, the net spectral line shifts associated with these flows weigh heavily in the average net line shift since the line shifts associated with the impulsive nanoflare-generated motion fill a small portion of the total time relative to the flows. In addition, the velocities of the evaporating or condensing plasma are large enough to perturb the ionizational balance and thus the intensity response of transition region spectral lines to variations in the plasma state caused by nanoflare-generated acoustic waves.

5.3. Ionization State

The ionizational state of a radiating ion in a time-varying atmosphere is determined by the characteristic times for recombination and collisional ionization. For ions such as O IV, the rates are sufficiently large one may consider them in ionizational equilibrium on the time scales of the acoustic waves considered. On the other hand, the helium-like ions C V and O IV, and the lithium-like ions N IV, and He-like recombination times on the order of 1 s. These rates are slow compared to the dynamic time scales associated with the dynamics in the transition region and thus lead to ionization states far from equilibrium. This nonequilibrium will, in turn, affect the concentration evolution of the ions ''neighboring'' the helium-like ions, primarily the lithium-like and, to a lesser extent, the beryllium-like ions. Let us use C IV as an example. Carbon, which is being heated or is flowing up through a temperature gradient from regions of T < 10^5 K to regions of T > 10^4 K,

will undergo successive ionizations and thus pass from beryllium-like C IV through lithium-like C IV and eventually to helium-like C V. The C IV is quickly ionized to C V as the temperature rises but the transition to C V is delayed by slow ionization rates. This C IV becomes overabundant at temperatures above its equilibrium temperature. The maximum concentration of C IV at the equilibrium value of x_{C IV} ≈ 0.4 to a value of x_{C IV} ≈ 1.0. In the reverse process, where plasma is cooling or flowing down to a lower temperature region, the recombination rate of C V to C IV is also is slow, C V remains the dominant carbon ion at temperatures far below the equilibrium value of T ≈ 10^5 K, while the maximum concentration is depressed to x_{C IV} ≈ 0.1.

In order to achieve net redshift in the absence of a net mass flow a correlation between the intensity and velocity line shifts in transition region spectral lines is necessary. The model calculations show that the redshifts in transition region spectral lines as a result of the correlation between the intensity and velocity that occurs in the loop cooling and material is condensing onto the chromosphere, while it is suppressed when the loop is heated and chromospheric material evaporates. This effect discriminates between lines from ions with short and long characteristic times of ionization. Model calculations also show that the computed average net line redshifts are smaller than the observed values. The net redshift in the computed model is for the reasons stated above largest as the loop plasma condenses onto the chromosphere. Increasing the average net shift should therefore be possible in loops where the plasma cools quickly and a large fraction of time is spent in the condensing phase. The cooling of coronal loops decreases with decreasing loop length and increasing base pressures. In order to match the observed net redshifts, the proposed mechanism of creating net redshifts may therefore require that most of the transition region emission predominantly come from loops shorter than the loop considered.

6. CONCLUSIONS

We have considered the hypothesis that compressive waves with origin in coronal nanoflares can account for the pervasive redshift seen in transition region lines. The calculations show that we are able to account for several of the observational characteristics of transition region spectral lines, including the apparently small correlation between intensity and the shift, the range of observed velocities, the correlation between line widths, shifts, and intensity, and the smaller net shift speeds in higher temperature lines. However, the average net shifts calculated in the present model are smaller than the observed values. This problem may be rectified by having the emission predominantly come from loops shorter than the loop considered.

The inclusion of a heating term for the low-temperature "chromospheric" plasma is a strict assumption that the upper chromosphere and lower transition region and corona are heated by different mechanisms than the corona. The role of the chromosphere in the heating in the model is mainly to set the chromospheric scale height H_c. A secondary role of the chromospheric heating lies in determining the rate of evaporation of chromospheric material as described in § 5. It is thought that the lower chromosphere is heated by the dissipation of acoustic waves. If these waves do not reach the transition region and corona with sufficient amplitude to cause significant heating has been pointed out by Athay & White (1978). In fact, the preponderance of redshifts in the transition region is an indication of the lack of upward propagating acoustic heating waves. We have made test calculations of acoustic waves propagating from the chromosphere to the transition region. Such waves cause a net upflow in transition region spectral lines. A complete theory of transition region line shifts should therefore also account for the fate of the acoustic flux presumably bearing the lower chromosphere. Here the role played by the ionization of hydrogen and the radiative losses from Ly_a (or from opacity critical).

The proposed hypothesis to explain the net redshift of transition region spectral lines is an alternative to the hypothesis based on steady flow through loops (e.g., Pneuman & Kopp 1977; Mariska 1983), or to the hypothesis of cooling, falling spicules (e.g., Athay & Holzer 1982; Athay or 1984). Separating the effects of these hypotheses each will require a thorough calculation of the observational consequences of each. A fundamental difficulty lies in determining the geometry of observed regions. It is important to know if one is observing two or more loops of anomalous at any given time or several "snapshots" of a cycle with period longer than the observational exposure time.

In any case the separation of steady state models from time-dependent episodic models should be fairly straightforward. A gas which is being repeatedly heated and cooled will reflect this behavior of emission lines originating from ions with differing characteristic ionization times. This should allow for future discriminate between Athay & Eder (1989) have made an analysis of transition region line shifts and found that observations of shifts are inconsistent with a continuous flow.

Calculations of loop flow by Spadaro, Antiochos, & Mariska (1991) where the effects of nonequilibrium ionization are included also seem to indicate that introducing the net redshift by continuous spicule flow through loops is difficult.

The difference between the spicule and the nanoflare models are somewhat more subtle as both rely on heating and cooling gas cycles. The hypothesis differ in where energy is deposited. In spicule scenarios, energy is inserted toward the footpoints of loops, and the net redshift is obtained by considering the time scales of heating and cooling regions that the plasma is longer in the falling, cooling phase than in the rising, heating phase. This asymmetry gives a net observed downflow of the gas at transition region temperature. Emission from C IV favors gas that has a "cold" history. It is not clear that the additional time spent in the cooling phase is enough to cause a net redshift. The calculations presented here show the opposite effect, but it is difficult to discard the spicule hypothesis

without seeing a detailed account of its observational consequences.

Episodically heated loops will, to remain in force and energy balance, adjust their mass content by alternately evaporating chromospheric plasma while being heated and by condensing matter onto the chromosphere while cooling. These processes lead to flows through the transition region. By themselves, these flows will not result in any significant net shift of transition region spectral lines. We have shown that a net redshift is possible to achieve if compressive waves are generated in the corona as a result of the heating by nanoflares. In addition to generating the slow mode waves studied in this paper, nanoflares also generate Alfven waves which will further broaden the line profiles. Fast-mode waves are also generated. These are reflected into regions of low Alfven speed and may also lead to modifications of the line profiles.

APPENDIX

1. NUMERICAL METHOD

The equations describing mass, momentum, and energy conservation as well as the ionizational state of the plasma are solved numerically by setting them on a discrete spatial and temporal grid. We have chosen to formulate the equations on an adaptive grid using the Reynolds transport theorem as outlined in Winkler, Norman, & Mihalas (1983). This allows us to resolve the transition region temperature gradient as well as to follow any shock propagation shocks or discontinuities in the solution. According to this formulation equations (1)–(4) may, in general, be written as

d_t(ρAz) + d_z([ηu_{el}]) + (S Δz)^n = 0 .                    (A1)

Here f represents the conserved quantity, S any sources or sinks, u_{el} = u - u_{grid} the difference between the material velocity and the grid velocity, while Δz is the size of a computational cell. The operators d_t and d_z work on the discrete quantities at a time step n and grid position i so that

d_t ≡ (u_i^{n+1} - u_i^n)/Δt ,     d_z ≡ (u_{i+1}^n - u_i^n) ,                    (A2)

where Δt is the length of the time step. The averages appearing in equations (A1) and (A2) are computed according to the Crank-Nicholson (or "θ") method so that at grid position i

u^n ≡ θu_{i-1}^{n+1} + (1 - θ)u_i^n ,     0 ≤ θ ≤ 1 ,                    (A3)

when computing the solution for time step n + 1. In order to maintain accuracy and stability particle and momentum fluxes (μ_{el}) are computed by the second-order upwind method of Van Leer (1974). This entails that the equations couple to the two nearest neighboring grid points on either side, leaving a block pentadiagonal system to be solved.

The grid density "ρ_z (number of grid points per unit distance) is computed by the method of Dorfi & Drury (1987), which essentially sets

ρ_z(z) ∝ R(z) ≡ √(1 + (z_σ d_z d)^2) ,                    (A4)

where R is the required resolution, f again represents any quantity, z the distance and the subscript "av" some appropriate average of the subscripted variable. In practice, we include information on the variation of the second derivatives and the maximal allowed variation of the grid density with respect to space and time. Solving this equation simultaneously with the dynamic equations allows us to resolve and follow large gradients in the solutions as well as to resolve the transition region temperature gradient.

The conservation equations along with the grid equation are solved via the conservation equations along with the grid equation are solved via the Newton-Raphson method, iterating until the calculated correction to our solution is less than some preset value – typically at a relative correction of 10^-6. To ensure a continuous solution we introduce artificial viscosity in equation (3). Following von Neumann & Richtmyer (1950) we write

Q = {[3μ_l^2(∂u/∂z)^2 ,      for (∂u/∂z) < 0,
    {0 ,                        for (∂u/∂z) ≥ 0,                    (A5)

where μ_l is chosen to be some fraction of the average grid spacing. The artificial viscosity will give a shock thickness of order l_s while reproducing the correct jump in the dynamic variables, as well as the correct propagation speed of the shock. It turned out that in the models presented in this paper the numerical viscosity was unimportant in both the energy and momentum balance.

When the system evolves in time compressive waves are generated. They propagate along the loop and should pass through the boundaries without reflection. We have implanted transparent boundary conditions by the method of Korevaar & Van Leer (1988): the physical variables are extrapolated outside the numerical grid using the characteristic variables. We find that the method gives a reflection of only a few percent of the outgoing wave energy, as long as the thermal conduction flux is not a dominant term in the energy equation. This condition is satisfied in the cool chromospheric plasma at the loop footpoints, and the boundaries do not significantly perturb our solutions.

The rate equations for elements other than hydrogen and helium couple to the dynamic equations mainly through the radiative loss term. A good approximation of the radiative loss term requires equations to be solved simultaneously from 4, for a time-independent precomputed radiative loss term, to 37, for a plasma considering of hydrogen, helium, oxygen, carbon, and neon, and thereafter, separately, the rate equations for the remaining elements. The radiative loss has been on the basis of the loss and density dependence to ensure good convergence and improve of the ion populations with the dynamic variables included when solving the dynamic equations. We find that 3 to 4 iterations are required at each time step.

The length of each time step is determined on the basis of the last time step, the Courant condition at the boundaries, and the change in the variables from the last time step. We find that the shortest time steps occur when shock waves are passing through the boundaries. In these cases the grid points must track through the pulse structure instead of following it.
